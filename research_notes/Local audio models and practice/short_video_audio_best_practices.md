# Music and sound design in short-form social video: evidence and best practices (Nov 2026)

Scope: what a small data-driven community brand (@myspeedpuzzling, ~2700 followers; animated data Reels 15–30 s and a 16 s weekly-recap Story; baked-in own music beds + SFX, no Instagram library music, no credits) should do with audio.

Evidence labels used throughout:
- **[Strong]** peer-reviewed experiment or meta-analysis, or large first-party platform data with stated method
- **[Moderate]** platform-commissioned or industry research with a stated method but not peer-reviewed, or a single peer-reviewed study in a different context
- **[Weak]** vendor/agency blogs, stat roundups, figures without a traceable primary source
- **[Opinion]** practitioner advice, no data
- (older) = published before 2022

Research was done on 16 Nov 2026 by web search; several items are known only from abstracts or secondary summaries, and that is noted per item.

## 1. Academic research: music tempo/energy/valence, music–visual sync, effects on attention, recall, sharing

### Takeaway
There is little direct research on background music in short social videos, and the findings are mixed. The best experiment (JCMC 2024, n≈620) found that fast-tempo instrumental music (120–160 bpm) made expert explainer TikToks more persuasive, probably by distracting viewers from counterarguing, with no effect on memory. Research on multimedia learning warns that background music can hurt *comprehension* of demanding content but usually does not hurt simple recall. Cutting on the beat is common practice with almost no modern evidence that it changes behaviour, and the "40% higher completion" figure people quote has no source. For data Reels, the evidence supports an upbeat, lyric-free bed that stays out of the way of the numbers. It does not support claims that music by itself drives shares.

### Cited Findings
- **Tempo and persuasion [Strong for its context].** Li, Li & Yang, "Correction by distraction: how high-tempo music enhances medical experts' debunking TikTok videos", *J. Computer-Mediated Communication* 29(5), 2024. Online 2×3 experiment (expert vs layperson video × no / low-tempo 60–90 bpm / high-tempo 120–160 bpm instrumental music), 623 participants in the correction conditions. Expert explainer videos with high-tempo music were best at reducing misbelief. High tempo helped expert/didactic videos but not testimonials. The proposed mechanism is reduced counterarguing (only partly supported, some effects marginal). **Tempo did not affect recognition memory.** Attention, sharing and credibility were not measured. Limitations: one topic, a specific population (75.8% women, family-planning U.S. adults), instrumental music only. — [OUP JCMC](https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/29/5/zmae007/7729237); [BPS Research Digest summary](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/upbeat-tunes-make-tiktoks-expert-debunkers-more-effective)
- **Stimulation vs engagement, inverted U [Moderate, preprint].** arXiv 2604.19995 (2026), "A Computational Model of Message Sensation Value in Short Video Multimodal Features…": 1,200 short videos coded and validated on N=14,492 videos from three platforms. Higher message sensation value (intensity of audio/visual stimulation) raises *sensory* engagement, but *behavioural* engagement (likes, shares etc.) follows an inverted U: "moderate MSV optimizes behavioral engagement." Only the abstract was read; which audio features were used is unknown. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19995)
- **Music in field content analyses is inconsistent [Weak–Moderate, correlational].** In 100 emergency-medicine TikToks, background music correlated *negatively* with engagement (likes+comments+shares combined); a dermatology TikTok study cited there found on-screen text + music + healthcare attire had the *highest* engagement. — [PMC11908529](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11908529/). An IRJEMS study using Spotify-style audio features found only weak positive correlations between play count and music duration/instrumentalness/liveness. — [IRJEMS](https://irjems.org/Volume-3-Issue-1/IRJEMS-V3I1P125.pdf)
- **Background music and learning/recall (multimedia learning) [Strong but conflicting].**
  - Rey (2012) meta-analysis of seductive details (39 effects): retention and transfer improve by *excluding* interesting but irrelevant additions; Mayer (2021) classes background music as an extraneous detail to exclude. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257690772_A_review_of_research_and_a_meta-analysis_of_the_seductive_detail_effect) (older)
  - Kämpfe et al. (2010) meta-analysis: overall negative effect of background music (older). A newer meta-analysis, de la Mora Velasco et al. (2023), reports a reliable small *positive* effect of background music on learning, larger when music plays during encoding. — [ResearchGate (2023 meta-analysis)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/369490840_The_impact_of_background_music_on_learners_A_systematic_review_and_meta-analysis)
  - de la Mora Velasco et al., *Frontiers in Psychology* 2017: background music did not affect recall but lowered comprehension; music works as a seductive detail for demanding processing. — [PMC5671572](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5671572/) (older than 2018, kept for context)
  - Lehmann et al. 2019 (*Applied Cognitive Psychology*): music did not predict recall and *improved* transfer. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/acp.3509)
- **Cuts on the beat [Weak].** The only direct test found is an old "On-Beat/Off-Beat" music-video study reporting attentional/cognitive differences between on-beat and off-beat cuts; full text unavailable, roughly 20 years old. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/6909355_Audiovisual_synchrony_perception_for_music_speech_and_object_actions). The often-repeated "Facebook study: strong audio-visual sync = up to 40% higher completion" appears in a tool vendor's blog with no link to any study. — [OpusClip blog](https://www.opus.pro/blog/best-ai-beat-sync). An arXiv beat-sync paper only *asserts* that beat alignment boosts engagement, as background. — [arXiv 2506.18881](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.18881v1)
- **Cuts and attention generally [Moderate].** A marketing paper using frame-by-frame eye data: scene cuts raise attentional synchrony about 0.67 s later; visual complexity has a delayed negative effect. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/403157640_The_dynamic_effects_of_visual_complexity_and_scene_cuts_on_viewer_attention)
- **Audio-visual salient events synchronise audiences [Moderate].** Concert study: audio-visual performances produced higher inter-subject heart-rate synchrony than audio-only; synchronised orienting responses happened at section boundaries (salient structural events). Synchrony did not track self-reported engagement. — [PMC11776452](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11776452/). A *Scientific Reports* 2024 study found narrative, not low-level audio-visual saliency, drives cardiac synchrony when content has a strong story. — [Nature Sci Rep](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-73066-8)

### Inferences
- For count-ups and rankings (didactic, number-heavy), a moderately upbeat (~110–130 bpm), lyric-free bed is the best-supported choice: the evidence suggests it does not hurt recall of simple facts and may help acceptance. Lyrics compete with on-screen text (seductive-detail logic), so avoid them.
- "Moderate stimulation" beats "maximum stimulation" for behaviour: don't stack loud music, many SFX and fast cuts all at once.
- Syncing key visual events (a number landing, a rank reveal, a piece snapping) to musical accents is good craft and fits the "salient events orient attention" findings, but there is no hard evidence it lifts completion or shares. Treat it as craft, not a growth lever.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found that isolates music valence/energy on Reels-style *sharing* or *saves*.
- No modern controlled study of on-beat vs off-beat cutting in short-form video; the 40% completion claim is unsourced.
- The full text of arXiv 2604.19995 (which audio features matter) was not read.

## 2. Sound-on vs sound-off viewing (Reels, Stories, TikTok, Shorts) and captions/on-screen text

### Takeaway
Reels are mostly watched with sound on: Meta says over 75% (Nov 2024) and over 80% (Meta/Toluna study, 2024 data, published Dec 2025). The best first-party number for Stories is older: Instagram said about 60% sound-on (around 2019); higher figures in roundups don't trace to a source. So roughly 1 in 4–5 Reels views and up to 2 in 5 Story views have no sound, and the video must work fully muted, with audio as a reward layer. Meta's own creative research found that hooks using both audio and visual cues in the first 1–2 s, and messages delivered through both channels, do better.

### Cited Findings
- **Reels sound-on, Meta [Moderate–Strong, first party]:** "over 75% of Reels views on Instagram are sound on"; Meta advises including audio (voiceover, music, ASMR or SFX). — [Meta for Developers blog, 7 Nov 2024](https://developers.facebook.com/blog/post/2024/11/07/unlock-the-power-of-reel-ads/)
- **Reels sound-on, Meta/Toluna [Moderate]:** "over 80% of Reels played on Instagram each day are played with sound on"; Meta's creative strategist says the old sound-off guidance for feed ads doesn't apply to Reels. Same study (100 Reels-style ads × 65 creative variables, each shown to 100 people in a simulated Reels feed; DE, UK, IT, TR; 2024): speech + music together → 2.0× odds of a top-20% brand-interest rank; message delivered both in audio and visually → 1.8×; **audio + visual hook in the first 1–2 s → 1.5× odds of top-20% purchase intent**; brand and main message in the first 5 s → 1.7×. Ads only, not organic posts. — [PPC Land summary of Meta's Dec 2025 release](https://ppc.land/meta-and-toluna-study-finds-emojis-lift-reels-purchase-intent-2-5x/)
- **EssenceMediacom (June 2024) [Moderate]:** on average 80% of Reels viewed with sound on. — reported in [search summary of Performance Marketing World / Meta coverage](https://www.performancemarketingworld.com/article/1875803/delivering-impact-rise-reels)
- **Meta: Reels campaigns with music or voiceover delivered up to 13% more incremental conversions (Meta for Business, Dec 2025) [Weak, only seen second-hand].** — [Benly (third-party)](https://benly.ai/learn/meta-ads/creative-best-practices)
- **Stories sound-on [Moderate but dated]:** Instagram told Adweek about 60% of Stories videos are viewed with sound on (≈2019). — [Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/digital/should-brands-use-captions-and-subtitles-in-video-content-for-stories/). The "70%" (attributed to MediaKix) and "76.4% Nielsen" figures in stat roundups could not be verified [Weak]. — [Outfame](https://www.outfame.com/blog/instagram-stories-statistics); [amraandelma](https://www.amraandelma.com/top-instagram-stories-statistics/). A survey of 350+ people: 29% "always" and 56% "sometimes" watch Stories with sound. — [HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/instagram-story-sound-preferences) (older)
- **Muted viewing and captions [Moderate, older]:** Verizon Media + Publicis (2019): 69% view video without sound in public places, 25% in private; 80% of caption users more likely to finish a video when captions are provided; captions gave +8% ad recall, +13% brand linkage. — via [Adweek / roundups](https://www.adweek.com/digital/should-brands-use-captions-and-subtitles-in-video-content-for-stories/) (older; not mobile-Reels-specific)
- **TikTok [Moderate, platform-commissioned]:** Kantar for TikTok (US, Jan 2021): 88% of users *agree sound is vital* to the TikTok experience; 73% say sound makes them "stop and look" at an ad. This is attitude data, not a sound-on viewing rate, and is often misquoted as one. — [TikTok for Business](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en/blog/amp-up-your-brand-creativity-with-sounds-for-business-voice-clips); [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/tiktok-shares-new-insights-into-the-importance-of-sound-for-marketing-promo/601569/) (older)
- **Practitioner disagreement [Opinion]:** some agencies still say "design for sound off" (hook must work visually; voiceover in the first 3 s is wasted on muted viewers) — [Keepers Digital](https://keepersdigital.com/en-us/resources/paid-social/instagram-reels-ads/); others say Reels are audio-first and the first audio event in the first ~0.5 s matters as much as the first frame — [AdLibrary](https://adlibrary.com/posts/reels-ads).
- **Instagram recommendation eligibility:** Mosseri (Jan 2025) named watch time, likes and sends as the top ranking signals, and listed "add audio" among the basic requirements for recommendation; in March 2025 he said music on photo posts/carousels helps but isn't essential. Reported second-hand. [Weak–Moderate] — [Dataslayer](https://www.dataslayer.ai/blog/instagram-algorithm-2025-complete-guide-for-marketers)

### Inferences
- MSP's current approach (all text in the frame, numbers readable without sound) is correct and should stay: 20–25% of Reels views and maybe ~40% of Story views are muted. Audio should *add* to an already complete visual story, never carry needed information.
- Since most Reels viewers *do* hear the audio, a deliberate audio event in the first second (a hit, riser or snap SFX at frame 0, not a slow fade-in) is worth doing; Meta's 1.5× hook finding supports audio+visual hooks together.
- Not having a voiceover is fine; the "speech + music" 2.0× finding is for brand-interest ads, and MSP's text-in-frame style is a reasonable substitute.

### Gaps
- No current (2023–2026) first-party sound-on figure for Instagram Stories was found.
- No first-party sound-on rate for YouTube Shorts or TikTok viewing found.
- All Meta numbers are for ads, not organic posts from small accounts.

## 3. Sonic branding: audio logos, consistency, length, recognition

### Takeaway
Owned sonic cues are among the most effective brand assets in large ad datasets (Ipsos: sonic brand cues 8.53× more likely to appear in high-performing ads, yet used in only ~6% of ads), and recognition research favours short (≈2–3 s, about 5–6 notes), simple-but-distinctive motifs used consistently. The research comes from big brands with huge exposure; for a 2700-follower account the benefit is building a familiar "MSP sound" in regular followers, not mass recognition. Repetition follows an inverted U (liking rises, then wears out), so keep the signature fixed and vary the bed.

### Cited Findings
- **Ipsos, "The Power of You" (Sheridan, Feb 2020) [Moderate, large industry meta-analysis, older]:** Ipsos Creative Excellence meta-analysis of 2,015 US video ads. Owned assets (characters, sonic brand cues) beat borrowed culture (celebrities, licensed music); audio assets on average 3.44× more effective than visual assets; sonic brand cues 8.53× more likely to appear in high-performing ads; sonic devices used in only ~6% of ads. Licensed music reported at about 1.2× (secondary source). — [Ipsos PDF](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/ct/publication/documents/2024-01/Ipsos_Views_Power_of_You.pdf); [Ipsos article](https://www.ipsos.com/en-us/knowledge/media-brand-communication/power-you-why-distinctive-brand-assets-are-driving-force-creative-effectiveness); [the brandgym](https://thebrandgym.com/distinctive-brand-assets-marketers-are-missing-a-trick/)
- **TikTok citing Ipsos [Moderate]:** brand linkage and recall rise over 8× when distinctive brand sounds are used, versus other elements like slogans and logos. — [TikTok Evolution of Sound Vol. 1](https://www.tiktok.com/business/en-AU/blog/evolution-of-sound-volume-1)
- **System1 × TikTok (via vendor) [Weak]:** ~900 short-form ads, 92 000 consumers; sonic assets reportedly lifted brand awareness 191% after only 2 s of exposure. — [A-MNEMONIC](https://a-mnemonic.com/how-does-music-influence-brand-perception-the-science-of-sonic-branding/); [Stephen Arnold Music](https://stephenarnoldmusic.com/sonic-branding-ranked-1-for-brand-awareness/)
- **Length [Moderate/Weak]:** Wu et al. (2010, n=353) found no significant effect of sonic logo length on recognition (older; reported via vendor blog). SoundOut (industry) reports the best sonic logos last 2–3 s and contain the brand name (9× better attribution; accuracy drops to 18% without it). Krishnan et al. (2012): optimum around 5–6 notes (older). — [TADADAM](https://tadadam.com/en/blog/simple-or-distinctive-sonic-logo); [TADADAM 75% recognition](https://tadadam.com/en/blog/brand-recognition-by-sound-alone); [A-MNEMONIC](https://a-mnemonic.com/how-does-music-influence-brand-perception-the-science-of-sonic-branding/)
- **Complexity trade-off [Moderate; peer-reviewed status unverified]:** Silas et al. (2026, Goldsmiths/Max Planck), 1,860 participants, 90 commercial sonic logos: structural complexity *improves recognition* but *impairs recall from memory*. Known only via vendor blog. — [TADADAM](https://tadadam.com/en/blog/simple-or-distinctive-sonic-logo)
- **Placement [Moderate, older peer-reviewed]:** a happy-sounding sonic logo worked better at the *end* of an ad (a sad one at the beginning); tested logos were typically 2–3 s. — ["Small sounds, big impact"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/359553015_Small_sounds_big_impact_sonic_logos_and_their_effect_on_consumer_attitudes_emotions_brands_and_advertising_placement)
- **Review [Strong as a review]:** Spence & Keller, "Sonic branding: A narrative review at the intersection of art and science", *Psychology & Marketing* 41 (2024) 1530–1548: a brand's sonic identity is the sum of sound experiences across touchpoints, not one asset; the field remains "as much an art as a science". — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/mar.21995); [Oxford open access](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:a3db49b9-75e6-41c9-b31e-586b4f76b605/files/rt148fj539)
- **Repetition and wear-out [Strong for the general pattern]:** Montoya et al. (2017, *Psychological Bulletin*, 268 estimates from 81 articles): liking rises with early exposures, then falls (inverted U). Peretz et al.: a music mere-exposure effect appears after a single repetition and lasts. Ad-music research: jingles are the most irritating ad-music type; repetition follows wear-in/wear-out, amplified by how stimulating the music is; overly simple music starts disliked and stays disliked, complex music grows on people. Yalch (1991): a jingle helped slogan memory most after the *first* exposure. Unfamiliar brands' ads wear out faster. — [Peretz et al.](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03201171.pdf); [irritating ad music](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/303685286_Stop_this_music_What_are_the_antecedents_and_impacts_of_irritation_towards_advertising_music_and_how_to_prevent_it); [attitude toward ad music](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/239781321_Attitude_toward_the_advertising_music_An_overlooked_potential_pitfall_in_commercials); [Ad fatigue overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ad_fatigue) (mostly older)

### Inferences
- A 1–2 s MSP signature (about 3–6 notes or a recognisable "piece snap + chime") at the *end* of every Reel and the weekly Story, always identical, fits the evidence: short, owned, consistent, happy-sounding at the end. MSP can't sing its name, so tying the motif to the logo on screen at the same moment stands in for the "brand name in the sound" advantage.
- Keep a fixed small SFX palette (the same snap, tick and whoosh sounds) as part of the sonic identity across formats (Spence & Keller's "sum of touchpoints").
- For the weekly Story: the same bed every week risks wear-out among the most loyal viewers who see it 52×/year. Better: keep the signature and SFX constant and rotate among 3–4 beds in the same style (e.g. a monthly or seasonal rotation). This is an inference; no study tested a weekly social cadence.
- Expect small effects at MSP's scale; the 8.53× figure describes large TV/online ad campaigns, not a 2700-follower organic feed.

### Gaps
- No study of sonic signatures for small or organic social accounts.
- Silas et al. 2026, Wu 2010, Krishnan 2012 were not read in the original; numbers come via a sonic branding vendor.
- No published data from Amp or Sixième Son with method details was found in this pass (their reports are mostly marketing material).

## 4. Practitioner best practices: music per content type, loudness and mix, first-second hook, endings/loops, variety vs repetition, silence

### Takeaway
There are no official loudness targets for Instagram, TikTok or Shorts. The practical consensus is to master around −14 LUFS integrated with −1 dBTP true peak (YouTube turns loud audio down but doesn't raise quiet audio), check on a phone, and keep the important sounds above ~200 Hz because phone speakers barely reproduce lower frequencies. Craft guidance (mostly opinion) says: put an audio event at frame 0; duck the music under SFX at key reveals; make the end point back to the start for Reel loops (YouTube Shorts now counts every replay as a view); match the mood to the content; use silence or a drop as a deliberate contrast just before a reveal.

### Cited Findings
- **Loudness [Weak/Opinion, no official spec]:** many tool and guide sites list −14 LUFS / −1 dBTP for YouTube, Shorts, TikTok and Reels; YouTube lowers louder audio to −14 LUFS but does not raise quiet audio, so being too quiet is worse than too loud. — [audio2time](https://www.audio2time.com/audio-loudness-standards/); [videovolumebooster](https://videovolumebooster.com/tools/lufs-standards). Clyper notes none of the platforms publish a loudness target (YouTube's upload specs cover codec/bitrate only; nothing in Instagram's help centre). — [Clyper](https://www.clyper.io/guides/how-to-make-a-video-louder). Sources contradict each other on TikTok: "no in-feed normalization, −7 to −8 LUFS hits harder" — [SongBrain](https://www.songbrain.ai/guides/lufs-for-spotify-and-tiktok) — vs "in 2026 a −8 LUFS master ends up quieter than a −14 LUFS master on TikTok; Shorts strictest, Reels a bit more lenient" — [Mr. Vocal](https://mrvocal.com/posts/loudness-for-shorts).
- **Phone speakers [Opinion, consistent across sources]:** most phone speakers struggle below ~200 Hz and nearly all roll off by 100 Hz (some say <500 Hz); use saturation/harmonics so bass "speaks" in 150–500 Hz; keep low end mono (phones sum to mono); high-pass reverb returns at 200–400 Hz; tame harsh SFX with a small cut around 2–3 kHz; always check on a phone. — [LANDR](https://blog.landr.com/make-bass-audible-phone-speakers/); [Leapwing](https://www.leapwingaudio.com/blog/mixing-low-end-for-any-sound-system/); [sfxengine](https://sfxengine.com/blog/sound-effects-for-phone); [mastrng](https://mastrng.substack.com/p/mixing-for-phone-speakers)
- **First seconds [Moderate for ads]:** Meta defines the hook as "the text, visuals and audio" in the first few seconds; audio+visual hooks in the first 1–2 s gave 1.5× odds of top-20% purchase intent (Meta/Toluna). — [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/meta-and-toluna-study-finds-emojis-lift-reels-purchase-intent-2-5x/). Practitioner: the first audio event should land in the first ~0.5 s [Opinion]. — [AdLibrary](https://adlibrary.com/posts/reels-ads)
- **Loops and replays [Moderate, first party]:** since 31 Mar 2025, YouTube Shorts counts a view each time a Short starts or *replays*, with no minimum watch time; the old metric is now "engaged views" (excludes loops) and monetisation uses engaged views. — [YouTube Community post](https://support.google.com/youtube/thread/333869549?hl=en&msgid=333876562); [TubeBuddy](https://www.tubebuddy.com/blog/youtube-shorts-view-count-update-what-creators-need-to-know-about-the-new-metrics/). Creator guides list seamless loops (end flows into start) as a replay driver, but say loops should be judged by engaged views and average % viewed [Opinion]. — [Soundstripe](https://www.soundstripe.com/blogs/how-to-grow-on-youtube-shorts); [Everpop](https://everpop.app/blog/what-counts-as-a-view-on-youtube-shorts)
- **Watch time and sends as Instagram's top signals** (Mosseri, Jan 2025) [Weak–Moderate, second-hand] — so audio that holds viewers to the end and makes the post fun to send matters more than audio as such. — [Dataslayer](https://www.dataslayer.ai/blog/instagram-algorithm-2025-complete-guide-for-marketers)
- **Original audio [Weak]:** blogs claim Instagram favours original audio and that reuse of your audio by others spreads both Reels; no primary Instagram statement found. Business accounts have limited commercial music access anyway. — [Chartlex](https://www.chartlex.com/blog/marketing/trending-audio-instagram-reels-algorithm-2026); [TrueFuture Media](https://www.truefuturemedia.com/articles/instagram-reels-reach-2026-business-growth-guide)
- **Music per content type [Opinion, plus Section 1 evidence]:** high-tempo instrumental music suited didactic/expert content in the JCMC experiment; the ad-music literature warns highly stimulating music wears out faster. No credible practitioner source with data on "tension/countdown vs celebration" scoring was found.

### Inferences (recommended practice for MSP, built from the above)
- **Master:** −14 LUFS integrated, −1 dBTP, for every platform; don't chase loudness. Test one upload per platform and compare by ear against a reference Reel.
- **Mix:** music bed about 6–10 dB under the SFX at reveals (sidechain duck on each number land/snap); SFX carry the "data moments"; keep content mono-safe and audible on a phone (energy in 200 Hz–5 kHz; saturate kicks/bass).
- **Content-type moods (craft judgement):**
  - Count-up / stats: steady upbeat pulse (~110–125 bpm), ticking SFX that speed up with the counter, a hit when it lands.
  - Rankings / leaderboards: building tension (rising pads/risers), a short drop to near-silence just before #1, then a celebratory hit + signature.
  - Celebration / records: brighter major key, a fuller hit, the signature at the end.
  - Calm explainer / "how it works": low-energy bed, sparse SFX.
- **Hook:** an audio transient at 0.0–0.3 s together with the first visual motion; no fade-in.
- **Ending:**
  - Reels: end the music on a phrase that resolves *into* the opening bar (same key and tempo; the last beat leads into the first), so the auto-loop feels seamless.
  - Story (auto-advances after the frame): end on a clean resolved button and the signature, finishing before the frame ends, rather than a loop.
- **Silence:** use short (0.3–1 s) drops before a reveal as contrast; never a silent opening, because sound-on viewers decide in the first second.
- **Variety:** a fixed signature + SFX palette, rotating beds (3–4 per format), and a new bed for special series/campaigns (e.g. Advent).

### Gaps
- No official loudness documentation from Instagram, TikTok or YouTube Shorts; TikTok behaviour is disputed, so an empirical upload test is advisable.
- No data-backed source on music choice per content type (data vs celebration vs countdown); recommendations are craft opinion.
- No credible research found on Story audio specifically (autoadvance behaviour, best ending).
- Motion-design studio guidance on SFX for data animation was not found in sources with data.

## 5. AI-generated music: audience perception, disclosure and authenticity (2024–2026)

### Takeaway
Listeners mostly cannot tell AI music from human music (Deezer/Ipsos 2025: 97% failed a blind test), and *without* a label AI music can be liked as much or more. But disclosure or perceived AI involvement reliably lowers perceived authenticity, trust and purchase intent in advertising research. 80% say fully AI music should be labelled. For MSP: a lyric-free background bed is a low-attention element, so the risk is low; the main risk is the community discovering it and feeling misled, given the "no credit" choice. Platform AI-labelling rules should be checked separately (not covered here).

### Cited Findings
- **Deezer × Ipsos (Nov 2025) [Moderate, large survey]:** 9000 people in 8 countries; 97% couldn't tell fully AI-generated songs from human ones in a blind test; 80% think 100% AI music should be clearly labelled; 45% of streaming users would like to filter it out; only 11% think it should be treated equally on charts. — [Deezer newsroom](https://newsroom-deezer.com/2025/11/deezer-ipsos-survey-ai-music/); [Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/science-technology/2025/11/13/are-you-listening-to-bots-survey-shows-ai-music-is-virtually-undetectable)
- **MIT Media Lab, Lecamwasam & Chaudhuri (arXiv 2506.02856, 2025) [Moderate, preprint]:** with labels varied, participants *rated* human-composed music as more emotionally effective regardless of label, but measured emotional responses did not differ, and participants more often *preferred* the AI-generated music. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.02856); [MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/exploring-listeners-perceptions-of-ai-generated-and-human-composed-music-for-functional-emotional-applications/)
- **Framing effects [Weak, thesis]:** true vs false AI/human disclosure of the same piece significantly changed evaluations. — [UCLouvain thesis](https://thesis.dial.uclouvain.be/bitstreams/435e907d-db65-4dde-9f80-1ef092bcc591/download)
- **IACIS 2025 [Weak–Moderate]:** respondents struggled to identify human vs AI vs co-created tracks, supported clear labelling, and preferred human–AI co-created music over fully AI music. — [IACIS](https://iacis.org/iis/2025/4_iis_2025_102-117.pdf)
- **AI disclosure in advertising [Moderate–Strong, several peer-reviewed studies]:**
  - Ads perceived as AI-made were seen as less authentic; disclosure lowered purchase intent and brand trust, more so among people who know AI tools; the effect was weaker for a fictitious, unknown brand. — [JISEM 2025](https://jisem-journal.com/index.php/journal/article/download/2659/1056/4303)
  - A "transparency paradox": the same AI ad labelled as human-made got higher trust. — [Equilibrium / economic-policy.pl](https://economic-policy.pl/index.php/eq/article/view/4038)
  - AI disclaimers lowered trust in the ad and the organisation, via persuasion knowledge. — [J. Interactive Advertising 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15252019.2025.2554149)
  - Mixed: disclosure raises perceived novelty but lowers authenticity (Shi & Jiang 2026). — [SAGE Open](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21582440261417793)
  - Kirkby et al. 2023: no effect of AI disclosure on brand attitude or authenticity. — cited in the same literature
  - Schilke & Reimann: across 13 experiments, AI disclosure consistently reduced trust (cited second-hand).
- **Unverified claim:** a 2026 arXiv essay says Deezer found engagement "dropped sharply" when listeners were told a track was AI. Deezer's own release does not show this; treat it as unsupported. — [arXiv 2606.26114](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.26114)

### Inferences
- An instrumental, low-salience bed under data visuals is the lowest-risk use of AI music: audiences can't detect it, and the research penalties attach mostly to *disclosed* AI in *focal* creative content.
- The authenticity penalty is larger for known brands and for people who know AI tools. A tight hobby community that values human effort (speed puzzling) may react to discovery more than average. Practical hedges: human-curated selection and editing (the "co-created" framing was preferred in IACIS), a human-made or human-edited signature motif, and an honest answer if anyone asks.
- Whether to proactively disclose is a values/policy decision for the owner; the evidence says disclosure costs a little trust, while being discovered hiding it is likely worse (not directly tested).

### Gaps
- No study found on AI *background* music specifically in social/community video, or on audience reactions when AI music use is discovered later.
- Platform rules (Meta/TikTok/YouTube AI-content labels for synthetic audio as of Nov 2026) were not researched here.
- Bain & Company Dec 2025 report ("In an AI Age, People Still Want the Radio Star") was cited second-hand only and not verified.
